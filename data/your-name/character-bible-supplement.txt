CHARACTER BIBLE SUPPLEMENT
The Your Name AU — Watching Room, Character
Dynamics & Continuity Rules
Attack on Titan | Erwin Smith / Zeke Yeager Body-Swap AU

This document supplements the existing Character Bible for a New Attack on Titan
Reaction Fic and the separate Zeke Yeager Character Analysis & Writing Reference.

It does not replace either document.

The original character profiles remain the primary reference for the Sacred Twelve. This
supplement establishes the changes specific to this AU: the Erwin–Zeke body swap, the
altered relationship status of Erwin and Levi, Zeke's addition to the watching room, and the
rules governing what each character knows.




PART I — THE STORY'S TWO LAYERS
1. The watching room and the story on screen are
separate realities
There are two distinct groups of characters.

The watching room contains the original twelve characters, plus Zeke as a thirteenth
occupant.

The story on screen follows an alternate-universe version of events in which Erwin Smith
and Zeke Yeager repeatedly exchange bodies.

These groups must never be treated as though they share the same experiences or
information.

The characters in the room watch the events unfold. They do not participate in them,
communicate with the people on screen, or automatically know what is happening beyond
what the screen has shown.

They should discover the AU at the same pace as the reader.

Their reactions should develop naturally as they accumulate information, reconsider
assumptions, and begin to understand the people on screen.
2. The Sacred Twelve
The original twelve occupants are:

   1.​ Petra Ral
   2.​ Erwin Smith
   3.​ Hange Zoë
   4.​ Levi Ackerman
   5.​ Ymir
   6.​ Historia Reiss, currently known as Christa
   7.​ Sasha Blouse
   8.​ Connie Springer
   9.​ Jean Kirstein
   10.​Mikasa Ackerman
   11.​Armin Arlert
   12.​Eren Yeager

Zeke Yeager is added as the thirteenth occupant.

The original twelve remain the central group. Zeke's arrival changes the dynamics, but the
story should not become a story exclusively about his integration.


3. The room's timeline
The watching room takes place approximately two days before the 57th Expedition.

The original character bible's timeline restrictions remain in effect.

At this point, the occupants do not know:

   ●​   That Annie, Reiner, and Bertolt are enemies.
   ●​   That Ymir is a Titan.
   ●​   That Christa is Historia Reiss.
   ●​   The true identity of the royal family.
   ●​   That humanity exists outside the Walls.
   ●​   That Marley exists.
   ●​   That Levi is an Ackerman.
   ●​   What is inside Eren's basement.
   ●​   The complete origin of the Titans.

They must not suddenly demonstrate knowledge acquired later in canon.

The addition of Zeke does not mean the others understand his world.

They do not know what Marley is, what a Warrior is, what the Eldian people are, or what
Zeke's position in his society entails.

Zeke's presence is itself an enormous mystery.
PART II — THE BODY-SWAP AU
4. The central premise
Erwin Smith and Zeke Yeager repeatedly exchange bodies.

The premise follows a Your Name-style pattern:

    ●​   Erwin wakes up in Zeke's body.
    ●​   Zeke wakes up in Erwin's body.
    ●​   Each experiences approximately one day in the other's life.
    ●​   The following morning, they return to their own bodies.
    ●​   The exchanges continue intermittently.

Neither initially knows the identity of the other.

This is important.

Erwin does not know Marley exists. Zeke has never been to Paradis.

Erwin has spent his life believing humanity is confined within the Walls. He knows nothing
about Marley, the Warriors, or the existence of Eldians outside the island.

Zeke has never visited Paradis and has never met Erwin, Levi, or any member of the Survey
Corps. He knows the island only through Marleyan education, propaganda, and military
information.

Neither man should possess knowledge that belongs exclusively to the other's world.


5. Immediate recognition of the exchange
They do not spend several days wondering whether something is wrong.

They realize almost immediately that they have experienced something impossible.

The difficulty is not recognizing that another person has occupied their body. The difficulty is
understanding who that person is, why it is happening, and how to survive the
consequences.

The first exchanges should be chaotic.

Both men are intelligent, observant, and accustomed to controlling their environments.
Neither is accustomed to waking up in a completely unfamiliar society, surrounded by people
who know them, with no reliable explanation for their circumstances.
Their intelligence helps them improvise, but it does not make them omniscient.


6. Zeke's first day in Erwin's body
Zeke wakes up in Erwin's bed.

Levi is asleep beside him, physically close, comfortable, and entirely familiar with the person
he believes is Erwin.

Zeke has no idea who Levi is.

He does not know Erwin's name, rank, relationships, military organization, or personal
circumstances.

His initial response should be confusion and an attempt to assess the situation.

He does not immediately interpret Levi's presence romantically. He does not know they are
together. He has no reason to know that this is an established relationship.

Levi, meanwhile, believes he is interacting with Erwin.

He may notice unusual behaviour, but there is no immediate reason for him to conclude that
Erwin's body is being occupied by a stranger.

Zeke must discover the world around him gradually:

   ●​   The military hierarchy.
   ●​   The Survey Corps.
   ●​   Erwin's position as Commander.
   ●​   The people who know and trust Erwin.
   ●​   The strange relationship between the military and the population inside the Walls.
   ●​   Levi's reputation and personality.
   ●​   The fact that he and Levi are privately together.

Zeke must not know about Humanity's Strongest before someone tells him.

He should discover Levi's reputation through an actual interaction or conversation. The title
should be unfamiliar to him.

His first day should contain a mixture of tension, improvisation, and situational comedy. He is
an intelligent man attempting to impersonate someone whose life he does not understand.


7. Erwin's first day in Zeke's body
Erwin wakes up in an unfamiliar world.

He does not know Zeke's name or identity.
He does not know Marley exists. He does not know the Warriors, the military structure, the
Eldian population outside the Walls, or the history of the conflict.

He is surrounded by people who expect him to behave like someone he has never met.

Erwin's response should be analytical and controlled, but he is not magically capable of
navigating an entirely foreign society without mistakes.

He will observe, ask carefully chosen questions, conceal his confusion, and attempt to
identify the political and military structures around him.

He may recognize propaganda, institutional contradictions, and the consequences of
militarization without knowing their historical origins.

His experience should be unsettling precisely because he cannot immediately establish the
boundaries of the world he has entered.


8. Communication between them
Initially, they cannot communicate directly.

They have no names, no contact information, and no reason to know how to find one
another.

Communication eventually develops through written messages left in the other's
environment.

This should be gradual.

Their first messages may be suspicious, defensive, and practical.

They are two strangers occupying one another's bodies. Neither should immediately trust the
other.

Their correspondence can develop through several stages:

   1.​ Establishing that the exchanges are real.
   2.​ Confirming that there is another person involved.
   3.​ Establishing basic rules and patterns.
   4.​ Exchanging limited information about their circumstances.
   5.​ Asking questions about the other's world.
   6.​ Becoming more candid as trust develops.
   7.​ Eventually discussing matters that are no longer purely practical.

Their written exchanges should reflect their personalities.

Erwin is deliberate, strategic, and careful with information.

Zeke is intelligent, dry, suspicious, and capable of being deliberately provocative.
Neither should immediately disclose everything.


9. The comedy of the premise
The early body-swap chapters should have genuine comedy.

Not because either man becomes stupid, but because intelligence cannot compensate for
the absurdity of their circumstances.

Both are experienced at reading people, but neither has the necessary context.

Zeke can be perfectly capable of manipulating a military conversation while having
absolutely no idea why everyone expects him to know a particular soldier's name.

Erwin can understand that a conversation is politically dangerous without knowing what the
political factions are.

Their mistakes should arise from missing information, not stupidity.

The humour should emerge from:

   ●​   Their attempts to maintain an unfamiliar identity.
   ●​   The contrast between their assumptions and reality.
   ●​   Other people noticing small inconsistencies.
   ●​   Their internal reactions to situations they cannot explain.
   ●​   The increasingly complicated consequences of what the other person did during the
        previous exchange.

The comedy should coexist with real stakes. The exchange is not a harmless inconvenience.




PART III — ERWIN AND LEVI IN THE AU
10. Their relationship is already established
This is a major change from the original character bible.

In the AU on screen, Erwin and Levi are already privately together.

They are not in an unconfessed-feelings stage.

They have chosen each other. Their relationship is established, comfortable, and lived-in.

They do not openly announce it to the military or make a public display of their relationship.
However, anyone paying attention can probably tell.
The relationship should feel like something that has existed for a while.

They are familiar with one another's habits, preferences, moods, and boundaries.

Their intimacy is not limited to sexual or romantic moments. It is part of their ordinary life.

Examples include:

   ●​   Levi stealing Erwin's shirts.
   ●​   Erwin knowing exactly how Levi takes his tea.
   ●​   Levi falling asleep against Erwin.
   ●​   Casual physical contact that neither needs to acknowledge.
   ●​   Familiar arguments about food, sleep, work, and injuries.
   ●​   Levi recognizing when Erwin is avoiding a subject.
   ●​   Erwin recognizing when Levi needs space rather than conversation.
   ●​   Small gestures of affection that are automatic rather than theatrical.

Their relationship should not require constant declarations to prove that it exists.


11. Levi's characterization within the relationship
Levi remains reserved, blunt, and occasionally difficult.

However, he is not emotionally illiterate.

He understands Erwin extremely well.

He can recognize subtle changes in Erwin's behaviour, tone, and mood. His familiarity with
Erwin is precisely what makes the body swap potentially noticeable.

Levi's affection should not turn him into a different person.

He can be affectionate without becoming excessively sentimental. He can tease Erwin,
complain about him, touch him casually, and remain entirely himself.

He is not constantly hostile toward the person he loves.

His relationship with Erwin includes trust, comfort, humour, and ordinary domestic familiarity.


12. What Zeke experiences through their relationship
Zeke's first experience of Levi's affection is accidental.

He is occupying Erwin's body, and Levi believes he is touching, speaking to, and sharing a
bed with the person he loves.

Zeke does not initially understand what he is witnessing.

Over time, he begins to recognize that Levi's affection is habitual and specific.
Levi knows Erwin.

He trusts him.

He is comfortable with him in ways that cannot be explained by professional loyalty.

This becomes an important part of Zeke's emotional development.

However, several distinctions must remain clear:

   ●​   Levi's affection for Erwin is not affection for Zeke.
   ●​   Zeke is initially experiencing intimacy that belongs to another relationship.
   ●​   Zeke's attachment to being treated with familiarity is not automatically romantic love.
   ●​   His eventual feelings for Levi are separate from the affection Levi mistakenly directs
        toward Erwin's body.

Zeke's progression should be slow:

Disorientation → curiosity → recognition → attachment to the experience of
familiarity → guilt → genuine feelings → acceptance of reality.

He may discover that being treated as someone who is known and wanted is more affecting
than he expected.

He may begin to wish that the affection were meant for him.

That does not mean Levi has done anything wrong.

Levi loves Erwin. That love remains real and central.

Zeke's eventual feelings must not turn Levi into a prize, a substitute, or an object to be won.


13. Erwin and Levi's relationship must remain
important
Do not diminish Erwin and Levi's relationship to make Zeke's emotional arc easier.

Their love is not an obstacle that must be removed.

It is a real relationship with its own history, affection, disagreements, and mutual
understanding.

Zeke's feelings do not invalidate it.

Levi should not casually fall in love with Zeke because Zeke has occupied Erwin's body.

The emotional difficulty comes from the fact that Zeke experiences something meaningful
without being the person to whom it belongs.

This should be handled with restraint and emotional honesty.
PART IV — ZEKE AS THE THIRTEENTH
OCCUPANT
14. Zeke's arrival
Zeke does not enter the watching room as a welcomed guest.

He is an enemy.

The twelve occupants have no reason to trust him, and he has no reason to expect them to.

His arrival should create immediate tension.

The original twelve have never met him. They do not know his name, his origins, or his
relationship to the Titans.

They have no concept of Marley.

They cannot immediately understand what he represents.

Zeke, on the other hand, is suddenly surrounded by people from a world he has only known
through Marleyan accounts and military information.

This is not a friendly introduction.

It is a confrontation between people who have every reason to distrust one another.


15. Zeke's initial attitude
Zeke should retain his canon personality.

He is intelligent, observant, cynical, dryly humorous, and capable of manipulating a
conversation when necessary.

He is not a harmless comic-relief character.

He is not immediately apologetic.

He does not beg for acceptance.

He understands that the people in the room hate him or will eventually have reasons to hate
him.

He may find their hostility predictable, irritating, or even amusing at times.
His humour can be a defence, a means of control, or simply a genuine part of his
personality.

He should not constantly deliver philosophical speeches or sarcastic one-liners.

His intelligence is not omniscience. He can misunderstand people, make mistakes, and be
genuinely surprised.

He is capable of curiosity and attachment, but these qualities do not erase his ideology or his
actions.

Understanding Zeke is not the same as excusing him.


16. Levi and Zeke
Levi's hostility toward Zeke should be especially pronounced.

Their canon history and conflict matter.

Levi has no obligation to be polite, forgiving, or interested in understanding him.

Zeke should understand that Levi's hatred is personal.

Their interactions may involve sharp exchanges, hostility, suspicion, and uncomfortable
silences.

However, neither should be reduced to repeating the same argument.

Their dynamic should evolve as the story provides new information.

Levi may eventually become more willing to listen without forgiving Zeke.

Zeke may eventually understand that Levi's hostility is not simply irrational hatred.

Neither development should happen quickly.


17. Gradual integration
Zeke's integration into the group is a long-term development, not an immediate change of
atmosphere.

The room should not collectively decide that he is misunderstood after one emotional
revelation.

Instead, integration should emerge through repeated exposure and shared experiences.

Possible developments include:

   ●​ Armin engaging him in intellectual discussions.
   ●​   Hange becoming interested in his knowledge of Titans and unfamiliar technology.
   ●​   Jean challenging his assumptions and refusing to accept convenient explanations.
   ●​   Connie occasionally responding to his dry humour despite himself.
   ●​   Sasha forming opinions based on his actual behaviour rather than abstract
        arguments.
   ●​   Historia questioning the moral implications of his ideology.
   ●​   Mikasa remaining protective and suspicious.
   ●​   Eren reacting strongly to the implications of Zeke's identity and beliefs.
   ●​   Petra noticing the difference between understanding someone's motives and trusting
        them.
   ●​   Erwin recognizing his intelligence while remaining cautious.

These are possibilities, not mandatory character assignments.

Not every character needs to become Zeke's friend.

Not every conversation needs to produce progress.

Some interactions should remain uncomfortable.

The group can gradually understand him while continuing to oppose him.


18. Zeke and Erwin in the watching room
The relationship between the room's Erwin and Zeke is particularly important.

They are not the same individuals as the Erwin and Zeke on screen, but they are watching
versions of themselves become entangled in an impossible situation.

Initially, Erwin should approach Zeke with caution.

He is a commander and strategist. He will recognize that Zeke is intelligent and potentially
dangerous.

He should not immediately trust him simply because the screen depicts an unusual
connection between their alternate selves.

Zeke, meanwhile, may be curious about the man whose body he repeatedly occupies in the
AU.

Their intellectual rapport can develop gradually through observation, discussion, and
disagreement.

They may recognize similarities in their strategic thinking while remaining fundamentally
opposed in ideology.

Their eventual friendship should not be confused with political agreement.

They can understand each other without agreeing with each other.
PART V — HOW THE ROOM SHOULD
REACT
19. The room is a social environment, not a sequence
of reaction shots
The original character bible's individual profiles and relationship dynamics remain in force.

However, the group should feel like a real collection of people sharing a room.

Avoid giving every character a line after every major event.

Not everyone needs to comment on every revelation.

A scene can contain several active voices, while others listen, exchange looks, interrupt, or
remain quiet.

A good reaction passage often needs only three or four active voices.

The remaining characters should still exist physically and emotionally in the scene.

They can react through:

   ●​   A change in posture.
   ●​   A glance toward someone else.
   ●​   An interrupted comment.
   ●​   A quiet disagreement.
   ●​   A visible expression of discomfort.
   ●​   A decision not to speak.
   ●​   A small joke that breaks tension.
   ●​   A practical question that redirects the discussion.

Silence is a valid reaction.


20. Preserve individual characterization
The original character bible already establishes how the Sacred Twelve think, speak, and
interact.

Those distinctions should remain.

The familiar tendencies are useful, but must not become rigid assignments.
For example:

   ●​ Armin may identify a logical inconsistency, but he can also be emotionally perceptive.
   ●​ Erwin may think strategically, but he can be amused, uncomfortable, or personally
      affected.
   ●​ Hange may be fascinated by an unfamiliar phenomenon, but their curiosity should
      not erase concern for people.
   ●​ Levi may identify immediate danger, but he can also make jokes and understand
      emotional subtleties.
   ●​ Petra may offer comfort, but she is also an experienced soldier capable of tactical
      analysis.
   ●​ Jean may challenge an argument, but he can also recognize vulnerability.
   ●​ Sasha may notice practical details and emotional shifts.
   ●​ Connie can be funny without being unintelligent.
   ●​ Mikasa's protectiveness should not make her incapable of independent judgment.
   ●​ Historia's empathy should not make her passive.
   ●​ Ymir's cynicism should not make her incapable of caring.
   ●​ Eren's emotional responses should not make him incapable of thought.

Use the original profiles as the foundation. Do not reduce anyone to a single function.


21. Reactions should develop over time
The characters should remember what they have seen.

A revelation in one chapter can change how they interpret something several chapters later.

Their understanding of the AU should accumulate.

For example, when Zeke first wakes up beside Levi, the room may interpret the situation as
strange or potentially intimate.

Later, when they learn that Erwin and Levi are already together, earlier scenes acquire a
different meaning.

Likewise, the room's understanding of Zeke should change as they learn more about his
circumstances, his ideology, and his relationship with the alternate Erwin.

Do not have characters repeatedly rediscover information they already know.

Do not make them understand things before the screen gives them enough evidence.


22. Keep the humour alive
The story has room for comedy even when its themes become serious.

The original twelve have established relationships, habits, rivalries, and personalities.
They should still joke, bicker, tease one another, and occasionally get distracted.

Zeke's presence can create new comedic possibilities, especially because he is an outsider
who does not share their social context.

However, he should not become the group's designated joke.

The humour should arise from character and circumstance, not from making everyone
behave out of character.




PART VI — KNOWLEDGE AND
CONTINUITY RULES
23. Two separate knowledge systems
There are two different sets of knowledge.

The watching room:

   ●​ Knows only what the characters knew at the point they were taken from their original
      timeline.
   ●​ Learns new information by watching the screen.
   ●​ Can make reasonable deductions from evidence.
   ●​ Cannot automatically know the truth behind unexplained events.

The AU on screen:

   ●​   Erwin begins with knowledge of life inside the Walls.
   ●​   Zeke begins with knowledge of Marley and its military society.
   ●​   Neither begins with knowledge of the other's world.
   ●​   Their discoveries must happen through direct experience, observation, and
        communication.

These knowledge systems must not be merged.


24. No accidental omniscience
Do not give characters knowledge simply because the author or reader knows it.

Examples:

   ●​ Room-Erwin does not automatically know what AU-Erwin is thinking.
   ●​ Room-Zeke does not automatically know what AU-Zeke has experienced.
   ●​   AU-Zeke does not know Levi's reputation before discovering it.
   ●​   AU-Erwin does not know what Marley is.
   ●​   The Sacred Twelve do not know Marley exists before the screen introduces it.
   ●​   Zeke cannot explain the history of Paradis from personal experience.
   ●​   Erwin cannot explain the history of Marley from personal experience.

A character may form a hypothesis.

A hypothesis is not confirmed knowledge.


25. Maintain the distinction between observation and
inference
Characters can notice things before they understand them.

For example:

A room occupant might notice that Levi behaves unusually comfortably around Erwin.

That is an observation.

They might infer that the two are close.

That is an interpretation.

They should not immediately know the precise nature or history of the relationship.

Likewise, Zeke may notice that people defer to the man whose body he occupies.

He can infer that this man has authority.

He does not yet know that he is the Commander of the Survey Corps.

Let the reader and characters make discoveries together.




PART VII — LONG-TERM THEMATIC
DIRECTION
26. Erwin and Zeke's eventual alliance
The eventual alliance between Erwin and Zeke should be earned.
It should not happen because they exchange a few letters and realize they are both
intelligent.

Their correspondence may create intellectual respect, but their political and moral
differences remain significant.

They must eventually confront the fact that their worlds are connected by a conflict neither
fully understood at the beginning.

They may discover that continuing the conflict will destroy the people they each want to
protect.

Their alliance should emerge from a gradual change in understanding, difficult decisions,
and an increasingly clear recognition of shared stakes.

It is not an instant friendship.

It is not an uncomplicated reconciliation.

It does not erase their previous actions.


27. Zeke's emotional development
Zeke's emotional arc should not be reduced to falling for Levi.

The experience of occupying Erwin's life may expose him to a form of intimacy he has not
experienced in the same way.

He sees what it means to be known by another person.

He experiences the comfort of being expected, trusted, and included in someone's ordinary
routines.

This may challenge his assumptions about himself and about what he wants.

But his development must remain broader than romance.

He has an ideology, a history, a family, a relationship with his own world, and a complicated
understanding of human suffering.

His feelings for Levi are one part of a much larger character journey.

Do not make his entire purpose in the story to become a better person for Levi.


28. The central themes
The AU should explore:

    ●​ Identity and the relationship between the self and the body.
   ●​   The difference between knowing a person and knowing their circumstances.
   ●​   The assumptions people make about unfamiliar societies.
   ●​   The limits of intelligence when context is missing.
   ●​   Intimacy, trust, and the experience of being known.
   ●​   The difference between being loved and being mistaken for someone else.
   ●​   The moral consequences of ideology.
   ●​   The possibility of understanding an enemy without excusing them.
   ●​   The ways people change when they encounter lives outside their own worldview.

The body swap is the mechanism.

The relationships, political conflict, and emotional discoveries are the story.




PART VIII — FINAL WRITING RULES
   1.​ Keep the original twelve's character profiles intact unless this supplement explicitly
       changes something.
   2.​ Treat Zeke as the thirteenth occupant, initially an enemy and outsider.
   3.​ Preserve the watching-room timeline: approximately two days before the 57th
       Expedition.
   4.​ Keep the watching room and AU on screen as separate realities.
   5.​ Erwin and Zeke do not initially know one another's identities.
   6.​ Erwin knows nothing about Marley; Zeke has never been to Paradis.
   7.​ The body swap is recognized immediately, but its cause and participants remain
       mysterious.
   8.​ Zeke's first day in Erwin's body begins with waking up beside an unfamiliar Levi.
   9.​ Zeke does not initially know Levi, his reputation, or his relationship with Erwin.
   10.​Erwin and Levi are already privately together in the AU.
   11.​Levi's love for Erwin remains real, established, and central.
   12.​Zeke's emotional development is gradual and must distinguish borrowed affection
       from genuine attachment.
   13.​Zeke retains his intelligence, cynicism, ideological convictions, humour, and capacity
       for attachment.
   14.​Understanding Zeke does not require forgiving him.
   15.​Zeke and Erwin's eventual alliance must be earned.
   16.​Do not make every room occupant speak after every scene.
   17.​Let the group interact organically, with interruptions, arguments, jokes, silence, and
       changing opinions.
   18.​Characters may infer, speculate, or be wrong. They must not become omniscient.
   19.​Do not turn character tendencies into rigid roles.
   20.​Preserve the emotional and political complexity of the premise.


The most important principle
Nobody should understand the whole story before they have lived through it.

Not Erwin. Not Zeke. Not Levi. Not the watching room.

The pleasure of this story comes from watching intelligent, complicated people encounter a
world they do not understand, make imperfect decisions, and slowly discover that the people
they thought they knew—or thought they hated—are considerably more complicated than
they imagined.
